The issue: gender-based violence

Violence against women is widespread, and it is preventable. This page explains what it is, who it harms, why it continues and what works against it.

What it is

The United Nations describes violence against women as any gender-based act that causes, or is likely to cause, physical, sexual or mental harm. That includes threats, coercion and being deprived of liberty, in public or in private life.[1] Researchers most often measure two kinds: violence by an intimate partner, and sexual violence by someone who is not a partner.[1]

Gender inequality sits at the root. Violence is not random. It grows where power is unequal and where harmful norms treat control over women as normal.[1]

    Equality is prevention

    Empowerment is not only personal. WHO’s evidence points to five levers that reduce violence at scale.[1]

    There is progress to build on. Partner violence reported by Filipino women fell from 29% in 2008 to 18% in 2022.[5] That happened alongside stronger laws such as the Anti-VAWC Act and the Safe Spaces Act. It is proof that change is possible, and a reason to keep going. See the laws.

    Equality includes everyone

    Kapantay centres women’s experience because the global evidence and Philippine law on partner violence do. That is not the whole picture.

    Women and girls

    About one in three experiences partner or sexual violence in her lifetime.[1] RA 9262 protects women and their children, including women abused by a same-sex partner.[10]

    Gender-diverse people

    Lifetime violence against trans women is estimated at 41% physical and 30% sexual.[1] The Safe Spaces Act covers harassment based on gender, gender identity and expression.[12]

    Men who are abused

    Men abused by a partner can file a case under the Revised Penal Code.[10] The sources on this site centre on women, so it does not present prevalence data for men.

    Beyond violence: the wider gender gap

    Violence is one symptom of wider inequality. The Philippines ranks 20th of 145 economies on the World Economic Forum’s 2026 gender gap index, with 78.0% of the gap closed.[9]

      Political empowerment is the widest gap at 37.9%. The index measures economy, education, health and politics. It does not measure violence, so a high rank does not mean people are safe.[9]
